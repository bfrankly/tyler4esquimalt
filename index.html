A letter to neighbours

Hi Esquimalt,

My name is Tyler Russell and I am running to be your township councillor.

I want to build upon the work of Mayor Barb Desjardins and the current council and make Esquimalt even safer, more fun, more accessible, easier to move around in, and filled with more love and inclusion than it already is.

I imagine a low‑cortisol town, one that has, embedded in its design, a communication of love to its current citizens, its newcomers, and future generations. Here is what I want to do to get us there.

Tyler Saxe Point neighbour, dad of an almost‑five‑year‑old, apartment dweller, car share and transit user

The platform

Nine things I want to do

These are my starting points. The door‑to‑door conversations happening now will sharpen them, and I'll post what changes and why.

  1. Fix the daily traffic jam

    Fix the horrible daily traffic jam that much of our town has become.

    First proposal: the Esquimalt Free Ride pilot →

  2. Grow the places where we play

    Enhance the delightful zones and facilities where children, and our whole community, can roam, connect, learn and play.

  3. Make it easier to walk and ride

    Increase the walkability and transit‑ability of our community.

  4. Calm the car‑and‑people zones

    Reduce the stress of the car‑pedestrian, car‑child and car‑cyclist interface zones.

  5. Pace development with infrastructure

    Keep new development in line with the roads, pipes, parks and services that have to support it.

  6. Become a carbon‑removing town

    In addition to emissions reduction, enhance green space and managed community biomass production, and move toward pyrolysis, enhanced rock weathering and other technologies in our green‑waste management, city works and urban design, so Esquimalt removes more carbon from the atmosphere than it puts in.

  7. Bring in more outside investment

    Work with staff to increase the grant funding, philanthropy and other external investment that goes into enhancing our community.

  8. Honour the Esquimalt and Songhees Nations

    Increasingly honour the Esquimalt and Songhees First Nations, on whose land we live, work and play, while deepening collaboration between the Township and local First Nations communities.

  9. Leave it better than we found it

    Leave our town safer, more inclusive, more bountiful, and universally smile‑filled than we found it.

Dispatches from the doorstep

Day one at the doorSeptember 2026Parking, child care, the right to cool, and the value of community stake in developments were the big issues on the first day of my door knocking.

Child care

Child care in general is a big challenge for young parents. The lack of child care spots was a general challenge noted by parents, with some noting how they have their young children on multiple waitlists as they struggle to balance work and child care. More specifically, lower‑income parents who work evenings and weekends noted the lack of child care services that accommodate their schedules. This was particularly acute for new immigrant families, who have limited local social networks and face linguistic, cultural and, let's call them bureaucratic‑maze barriers to accessing local services.

My reflection on this is that we need to better equip our community so that child care meets the diverse needs of our residents. This does not all have to be a municipal or other level of government responsibility or expense, but there is a big role for the municipality to play, in collaboration with other service providers, as an efficient facilitator, coordinator and advocate. If anyone has a specific proposal they would like to share, please let me know and maybe there is an initiative we can push forward.

Street parking

There were strong arguments that Esquimalt wildly lacks street parking, with the point made that if a major construction project were to come to town, it would squeeze residents out of their preferred homefront spots. Those homefront spots are clearly very highly valued!

One resident recounted how, when they moved into their Esquimalt home a number of years ago, if they happened to park in a spot right in front of their own house that turned out to be a neighbour's long‑time preferred spot, they would find bags of dog poop and grass clippings on their vehicle. Rather than communicate their preferences directly, the neighbour communicated via poop bags and yard waste. It was only when the issue was finally raised in person that they realised what was going on.

Some residents felt that other homeowners were guarding the street in front of their homes while filling their own driveways and front lawns with boats, RVs and other space‑occupying paraphernalia. Parking is clearly an issue for Esquimalt residents.

Getting to and from the base

One resident had an interesting twist on parking. They alerted me to the Base's policy of allowing free parking because Esquimalt has no pay parking. In Halifax, they noted, the base charges for parking on par with local rates. I looked it up, and it turns out it is a thing: DND's policy is to charge for parking at local market rates, and if the local market is free, it is free on base too. This resident suggested that bringing in nominal paid parking, perhaps with street‑specific passes for residents and paid parking for non‑residents, might have some impact on base employees' transit use. Though, they noted, bringing back the Blue Boat (the ferry from the base to Colwood) or reviving the Island Rail Corridor, at least to Langford, would be the real solution.

Another long‑time resident, born and raised here, who worked at the dockyard and is now retired, recounted how the Blue Boat took loads of cars off our constrained roadways. When the Blue Boat was privatised and became a paid service, it fell apart, and we started to get the congestion we know all too well today. I looked it up, and that does appear to be exactly what happened. Here is some follow‑on reading for anyone interested:

  • Celebrating what the Blue Boat provided, 2009 (page 10)
  • Blue Boat to cease, Lookout Newspaper
  • Goodbye Blue Boat service, Lookout Newspaper
  • Solve the Colwood Crawl, Capital Daily, 2022

The Capital Daily piece is a particularly interesting and informative overview of the breadth of proposals that have come along. Reading it made me reflect that no matter how much highway widening you do, it isn't going to have much impact on the stretch of Admirals that matters most to residents: the bit from Esquimalt Road to the Island Highway. That stretch is exactly what my first proposal, the Esquimalt Free Ride, is aimed at.

The right to cool

The right to cool was my "you learn something new every day" piece, and I am grateful to the resident who brought it to my attention. This one is important. As heatwaves become more and more a part of our lived experience, tenants' access to coolness is essential. It makes sense to me that no resident should be living with indoor temperatures above 26 degrees.

Ecotrust Canada's 2025 report lays out the sorts of bylaws municipalities can bring in. According to their website, New Westminster brought in a bylaw in June requiring landlords to keep at least one living space in an occupied rental unit at a safe temperature of 26°C or lower at night. That sounds abundantly reasonable to me, and I will work to table a proposal of this sort soon after taking office. If I am elected, hound me, and hold me and the rest of council to account on this. With an El Niño possibly on the way, there is a real chance that the summer of 2027 is the summer we need this bylaw in place.

Community stake in developments

One resident drew a very interesting contrast between two recent developments. The Proxima development, they quipped, was all about getting as close to the road as possible, eliminating not only old trees but also a bus bay, and making, in their view, the Lampson and Esquimalt Road intersection more dangerous. They contrasted this with the Esquimalt Town Square development where, they posited, because the community had a stake, an important old tree was kept and the space was made breathable and vibrant, a real addition to the community. This conversation was food for thought, and I think it makes sense for us to reflect on what role community stake can play in future developments that will call Esquimalt home.

I particularly like the notion of development as a collaboration between community and entrepreneurs, rather than a wrestling match in which a singularly focused developer wrangles as much as they can out of a multi‑focused municipality to maximise the profitability of their project. Finding paths, as with the Esquimalt Town Square, where the community shares a stake in a development is interesting to contemplate. This would take more study and consideration, but I wonder whether an approach where the community has a stake may be relevant when it comes time to redevelop the Country Grocer complex.

Small fixes

I also heard calls for stop signs at the complex intersection of Munro and Fraser, and a call from a young parent for greater traffic calming on Sturdee.

01 I'll advocate for this

The Esquimalt Free Ride

An Esquimalt Defence and Employment Mobility Pilot: free regional transit for people who work here, with more buses timed to shift changes.

The problem

Esquimalt hosts an extraordinary concentration of regional employment, including CFB Esquimalt, the home of Canada's Pacific Fleet, and the Esquimalt Graving Dock and Victoria Shipyards. Thousands of workers travel each day through a small number of constrained transportation corridors, contributing to recurring congestion on Admirals, Esquimalt, Lampson and connecting roads.

Significant expansion of roadway capacity would be costly, disruptive and unlikely to provide a durable solution.

The proposal

Esquimalt should seek provincial and federal support for an Esquimalt Defence and Employment Mobility Pilot, or an Esquimalt Regional Free Ride program.

For an initial 18‑ to 24‑month demonstration period, people employed in Esquimalt would be eligible for free regional transit, supported by increased service timed to major employment shifts and targeted transit‑priority improvements. An alternative would be to make the designated commuter routes along Admirals Road, Esquimalt Road and Lampson Street free for all riders.

The program would be undertaken in partnership with the Victoria Regional Transit Commission, BC Transit, the Capital Regional District, CFB Esquimalt, major employers and senior levels of government, with the option of extending it for up to five years if it demonstrates significant reductions in automobile commuting and congestion.

The project would test whether removing the cost of transit while improving its convenience can substantially reduce peak‑hour vehicle traffic without widening Esquimalt's constrained road network.

A second benefit for the base

For CFB Esquimalt in particular, the project would provide greater transportation redundancy and operational resilience for Canada's Pacific naval establishment. In an era of increasingly sophisticated asymmetric threats, natural disasters and other potential disruptions, reducing dependence on a small number of congested automobile corridors would strengthen the ability of defence personnel and other critical workers to reliably reach and leave their workplaces.

How we'd know it worked

Success would be measured against existing traffic counts and commute data, including changes in:

  • single‑occupancy vehicle trips
  • transit ridership
  • peak travel times
  • greenhouse‑gas emissions
  • employee commute reliability
  • cost per automobile trip removed

Paying for it

There are a number of established federal and provincial programs that Esquimalt could apply to in order to fund this. Bringing that outside investment into town is platform item 7.

About Tyler

An Esquimalt dad who knows the town at stroller speed, and now scooter speed.

My campaign started with my son. He is almost five, and he has been persistently insisting that something be done about the bumpiness of our community's sidewalks. I got to know the unique texture of the Saxe Point and Rockheights neighbourhoods first at stroller and more recently at scooter speed, and he has a point.

We moved here in 2021, and I am convinced that this town, daily traffic jam aside, is the best place to live for anyone who wants to raise children in a warm, convenient, walkable, park‑rich community with fun festivals and vibrant events. It is a place I will happily age into, with down‑to‑earth friends nearby and grocery stores and other services a short stroll away.

Before Esquimalt, I lived for extended periods on three continents, from an ethnically diverse farming village in eastern Croatia to some of Asia's largest and most complex cities. Along the way I interned at the Pearson Peacekeeping Training Centre, served as Coordinator for International Relations for a small Japanese municipality, where I coordinated affairs for the Cameroonian national football team for the FIFA World Cup, organized large‑scale art exhibitions in Korea, and ran the Okanagan's largest arts centre. More recently I have helped international partners build disaster‑preparedness training tools and consulted for environmentally progressive forestry transformation and waste‑management projects.

My son and I are regulars at our parks and recreation facilities, and I am grateful for the shops, restaurants and amenities close by. I see Esquimalt as a community where friends of all ages and backgrounds can have fun, feel included, know there is support when they need it, and be joy‑filled as we work together to make this an even better place to be.
